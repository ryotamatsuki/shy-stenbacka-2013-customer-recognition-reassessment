\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}

The closest literature has two roles in this reassessment: it determines what is already known about mixed pricing, and it clarifies what is specific to the correction of the inspected model.

A long literature shows that price dispersion or mixed pricing can arise when firms face heterogeneous groups with different information, loyalties, or captive status.  \citet{Shilony1977} studies mixed pricing in oligopoly, \citet{Varian1980} develops a canonical sales model with informed and uninformed consumers, and \citet{Narasimhan1988} analyzes promotional price competition.  These papers make clear that mixed pricing itself cannot be the novelty of the present analysis.

Customer recognition brings related forces into behavior-based pricing.  \citet{Esteves2010} analyzes pricing with customer recognition and a discrete distribution of consumer preferences, explicitly obtaining mixed pricing in part of the model.  \citet{deNijs2017} studies customer-information sharing in a behavior-based price-discrimination environment.  More recent work in \emph{Information Economics and Policy} continues this line: \citet{ColomboFilippiniPignataro2024} studies information sharing and personalized pricing in a collusive environment, \citet{Dubus2024} studies behavior-based algorithmic pricing with endogenous data use, and \citet{ColomboGrazianoPignataro2025} studies personalized pricing with imperfect customer recognition.  These papers confirm that the interaction of customer information, targeting, pricing, and welfare remains an active research area.  Our analysis belongs to this customer-information family but asks a different question: whether the terminal price profiles used in a particular multi-stage recognition-and-sharing model are global equilibria over their stated parameter domain, and what happens to the earlier stages when they are not.

The distinction is therefore between a mechanism family and a source-specific equilibrium statement.  We do not claim that a common price over heterogeneous customers creates a previously unknown economic force.  Instead, we show that in the inspected Shy--Stenbacka baseline the common-price constraint creates a piecewise demand geometry that invalidates a particular pure continuation over an admissible open interval.  The exact threshold, the absence of any alternative pure branch in that interval, and the backward-induction consequences are specific to this model.

This positioning also explains why we do not expand the paper into a general theorem about piecewise common-control games.  Stage-by-stage novelty searches found close prior mechanism families, and our own portability diagnostics show that the strongest pure-nonexistence conclusion does not survive two natural changes in primitives.  The appropriate contribution is therefore a transparent reassessment with an explicit boundary, not a relabeled general theory result.
